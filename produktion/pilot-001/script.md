At fourteen minutes past two in the afternoon, on the fourteenth of August,

## CHAPTER 1 — What we usually get told (0:35–2:00)

The version of this story most of us know is short: a power line touched a
tree in Ohio, and the lights went out from Detroit to New York.

[VISUAL: The popular version as a simple three-panel cartoon — tree, spark,
dark skyline. Deliberately crude, because we are about to take it apart.]

That version is not wrong, exactly. Lines did touch trees. Three of them, that
afternoon. [SOURCE: p. __]

But the investigation that followed grouped the causes of this blackout into
four. [SOURCE: p. __] Vegetation — the trees — is the third of them.

[VISUAL: The four grouped causes, listed in the report's own order:
inadequate system understanding · inadequate situational awareness ·
inadequate tree trimming · inadequate reliability coordinator diagnostic
support. Third item highlighted.]

Two of those four are not about hardware at all. They are about knowing.
[PAUSE] This is a story about information, and it has been told as a story
about landscaping.

We have not investigated anything here. Everything in this video comes from
the final report of the U.S.–Canada Power System Outage Task Force, published
in April two thousand four, and you can download it yourself.

[VISUAL: The report cover, with the citation on screen. This citation style —
document, page — appears throughout the video.]

## CHAPTER 2 — The grid is a balance, not a supply (2:00–4:00)

To understand what went wrong, you need one idea about power grids, and it is
not the one most people carry around.

A grid is not a reservoir that things are drawn from. It is a balance that has
to hold, continuously, everywhere at once. Electricity is generated and
consumed in the same instant. And the paths it takes are not chosen — current
distributes itself across every available route according to physics.

[VISUAL: A simple network of five nodes. Current shown as flow thickness.
One line is removed; the flow visibly redistributes to the others — nobody
decided this, it simply happens.]

[PAUSE]

Which gives us the mechanism for everything that follows. When a
transmission line drops out, its load does not disappear. It moves — onto the
lines that remain. Those lines now carry more current. More current means more
heat. Heat makes a conductor expand, and an expanding conductor sags.

[VISUAL: Side view of a line between two towers. Load increases, the
conductor warms, the curve deepens — and the gap to the tree beneath it
closes.]

A line that comfortably cleared the tree below it at two o'clock may not clear
it at half past three. Nothing moved. The line simply got warmer.

That is why "a tree caused it" is the wrong shape of explanation. The trees
did not grow that afternoon. [PAUSE] The lines came down to meet them.

## CHAPTER 3 — Akron, 14:14 (4:00–6:30)

[VISUAL: Return to the timeline. It will now run continuously in the corner
for the rest of the video.]

At thirteen thirty-one, a generating unit called Eastlake Five goes offline.
[SOURCE: p. __] In itself, unremarkable. Units trip. The system is built for
that.

At fourteen fourteen, the alarm processing system at FirstEnergy stops
functioning. [SOURCE: p. __]

[PAUSE]

Think about what an alarm system does in a control room. It is not a
convenience. Nobody watches a grid by reading every value continuously — there
are too many. You watch the exceptions. The alarm system is the thing that
decides what deserves your attention.

When it fails, the screens do not go blank. That is the part worth sitting
with. [PAUSE] They keep showing you a picture. The picture is simply no
longer being updated.

[VISUAL: **The two panels.** Left: the real state of the network, lines
beginning to drop out. Right: what the control room display showed. At the
start they are identical. As the timeline advances, the left panel changes
and the right panel does not. Let the gap grow in silence.]

And so, at five past three, the Chamberlin–Harding line ⟨CHAM-ber-lin⟩ touches
a tree and trips. [SOURCE: p. __] At thirty-two minutes past, Hanna–Juniper
⟨JOO-ni-per⟩. [SOURCE: p. __] At forty-one minutes past, Star–South Canton.
[SOURCE: p. __]

Each one moves its load onto the others. Each one makes the next more likely.
And in Akron, the display still looks like an ordinary afternoon.

The operators were not being careless. They were doing something much more
ordinary and much harder to defend against: they were trusting an instrument.

## CHAPTER 4 — 16:05:57 (6:30–8:15)

By shortly after four o'clock, the load pushed down onto the lower-voltage
network underneath — and that network begins to fail, line after line.
[SOURCE: p. __]

At five minutes and fifty-seven seconds past four, the Sammis–Star line trips.
[SOURCE: p. __]

[VISUAL: Timeline stops. Hold on the single line dropping out.]

This is generally identified as the moment the event stopped being a regional
problem. [SOURCE: p. __] After this, the sequence no longer needed anyone's
mistakes. Protective equipment across a much larger area began doing exactly
what it was designed to do — disconnecting to protect itself — and the
disconnection propagated.

[VISUAL: Outline map, Ohio through Michigan, Ontario, New York. Time counter
in seconds. The spread is fast and the counter should make that uncomfortable.]

Within roughly eight minutes it was over. About fifty million people, and
sixty-one thousand eight hundred megawatts of load, across eight U.S. states
and Ontario. [SOURCE: p. __] In parts of the United States, power was out for
four days. Parts of Ontario had rolling blackouts for more than a week.
[SOURCE: p. __]

## CHAPTER 5 — What was said, and what was found (8:15–9:30)

In the days afterwards, several explanations circulated widely.

That it was the Blaster worm — a piece of malware spreading through Windows
systems that same August. The Task Force examined that question specifically.
[SOURCE: p. __ — **exact wording required**]

That it was a lightning strike. [SOURCE: p. __] That it started in Canada.
[SOURCE: p. __] The sequence begins in northern Ohio.

[VISUAL: Each claim shown as a headline, then the report's finding placed
beneath it. No commentary in the narration — the pairing is the argument.]

What the report describes instead is slower and less satisfying: a control
room that could not see, a coordinator without the diagnostic support to catch
it from outside, vegetation management that left too little margin, and a
system understanding that did not match the system. [SOURCE: p. __]

Forty-six recommendations followed. [SOURCE: p. __]

## Title suggestions

1. *The Two Hours Nobody Was Watching* — leads with the decision point; no
   claim the video cannot keep.
2. *The 2003 Blackout Was Not Caused by a Tree* — states the report's own
   emphasis against the popular version. Higher CTR, slightly more combative.
3. *What the Control Room Saw* — quietest option; strongest if the channel
   already has an audience, weakest for a cold start.

## Thumbnail concepts (subject only — design is the designer's)

1. The two panels: real grid state versus control room display, at the same
   timestamp. Communicates the whole video without text.
2. A single line dropping out of a schematic, with the timestamp 16:05:57.
3. The sag geometry: conductor and tree, the closing gap.

## Open questions for research

1. **All page references.** Nothing may be published while `[SOURCE: p. __]`
   remains unfilled.
2. **The Blaster passage — exact wording.** "Did not cause" and "found no
   evidence that" are different claims. The script must reproduce whichever
   the report actually makes.
3. **The mandatory closing segment** cannot be written from the current brief.
4. **Sammis–Star framing.** "Generally identified as" is a hedge covering the
   fact that the report's own characterisation is unverified. If the report
   states it plainly, the hedge should go.
